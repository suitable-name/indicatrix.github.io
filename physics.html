Physics & optics

What Indicatrix actually computes, and where it lives in the source tree.

1What is modelled

The summary below is the whole model in one table; the sections after it take each row in turn.

2Hero-wavelength spectral sampling

RGB rendering assigns each ray one of three fixed colour channels, which cannot represent a continuously varying refractive index and produces banded colour fringing inside a dispersive gemstone. Independently sampling one random wavelength per ray avoids the banding but converges far more slowly, since each pixel's channels are decorrelated.

Indicatrix instead traces 8 wavelengths per ray at once (NUM_CHANNELS, crates/indicatrix/src/optics/raytracer/mod.rs): a hero wavelength λ₀ drawn uniformly over the visible range 380–780 nm, plus 7 companions spaced by a fixed fractional offset and wrapped into the same range (wrapped_hero_wavelengths, optics/raytracer/transport/mod.rs): 

lambda_min = 380, lambda_max = 780
lambda_i = lambda_min + ((lambda_0 - lambda_min + i * delta) mod (lambda_max - lambda_min))
delta = (lambda_max - lambda_min) / 8

All 8 channels follow one shared, hero-driven geometric path. At an interior dispersive event (an entry into the gem), a companion whose own refracted direction diverges from the hero's beyond a fixed cosine tolerance loses its radiance for the rest of the trace (DIRECTION_MATCH_COS_TOL, optics/raytracer/refraction/). At the bounce where the hero-driven path actually leaves the gem, a still-diverged companion is not simply dropped: it receives its own Fresnel transmission and a one-off environment lookup along its own refracted direction ("exit-event spectral splitting"), which reduces variance without introducing bias. The channels are then integrated into CIE XYZ with a spectral multiple-importance-sampling weight per channel: Veach's balance heuristic over exactly the hero choices under which that channel would have been alive on the realized path, its own compatibility family, which differs from channel to channel because the direction tolerance is a band around each hero (integrate_channels_to_xyz_families, optics/raytracer/color.rs). Production renders always run with exit splitting on, so this is the weighting they use. The single shared weight over all 8 hero choices (spectral_mis_weight) belongs to the no-splitting path, and is what the per-channel form reduces to whenever every family is the full set.

3Sellmeier and Cauchy dispersion

Rather than three fixed indices for red, green, and blue, each built-in material carries a dispersion model evaluated at the ray's actual wavelength (crates/indicatrix/src/optics/dispersion.rs, optics/materials/, one file per group of species).

3-term Sellmeier

n^2(lambda) = 1 + sum_i [ B_i * lambda^2 / (lambda^2 - C_i) ]   for i in {1, 2, 3}
(lambda in micrometres)

Used where a primary fit exists — Diamond (Peter 1923), Sapphire/Ruby (Malitson & Dodge 1972), Quartz (Ghosh 1999), Spinel, Cubic Zirconia, Moissanite, YAG, and the two reference optical glasses, among others. A fit with fewer than three poles (e.g. Diamond's original 2-term literature form) is stored in the same form by zeroing the unused pole(s), and a constant term is a pole at C = 0 (Moissanite, Rutile). DispersionModel::Sellmeier1, the single-pole variant, exists in optics/dispersion.rs; no built-in material uses it, but a custom material can (see below).

2-parameter Cauchy

n(lambda) = A + B / lambda^2   (lambda in micrometres)

Used as a lower-confidence fallback where no primary Sellmeier fit exists in the optics literature (several garnets, Zircon, Topaz, Tourmaline, Tanzanite, and others) — solved from a sourced n_D and a gemological dispersion figure converted from the Fraunhofer B–G convention to F–C. The underlying Cauchy model (optics/dispersion.rs) also carries an optional C/lambda^4 term; every built-in material sets C = 0, reducing it to the 2-parameter form above. Each material's source code comments cite the specific reference and confidence tier used.

Custom coefficients and their validation

A custom material made in the desktop studio can carry its own curve instead of a mean index and an F−C figure: a 1-term or 3-term Sellmeier fit, or a Cauchy fit with the C/lambda^4 term (GemMaterial::new_custom_with_dispersion, optics/materials/custom.rs). The evaluator floors any index below 1 at 1, so a bad curve would render a clear block without a hint why. The curve is therefore checked before it is used (DispersionModel::validate, optics/dispersion.rs):

  • every coefficient is a finite number;
  • no Sellmeier term has its resonance, the square root of C in micrometres, between 300 and 800 nm, where the index would blow up (C is a wavelength squared, so 0.01 puts a resonance at 100 nm);
  • the index is finite and above 1 at every 5 nm step from 380 to 780 nm.

A curve that fails cannot be saved. Three softer checks only warn (DispersionModel::warnings): the index rises with wavelength somewhere in the visible band, the Abbe number Vd = (nD − 1) / (nF − nC) lies outside 5–120, or the index is the same at the F and C lines, so the stone would show no fire. nD, nF and nC are read at 589.3, 486.1 and 656.3 nm.

4Stokes–Mueller polarized transport

Light inside a cut gemstone can undergo dozens of internal reflections before it exits. Treating reflectance as an unpolarized scalar probability misses the polarization state that builds up over those bounces, including total-internal-reflection phase retardation. Indicatrix represents light as a 4-component Stokes vector (optics/polarization.rs): 

S = [ I, Q, U, V ]
  • I — total intensity
  • Q — horizontal vs. vertical linear polarization
  • U — +45° vs. −45° linear polarization
  • V — right- vs. left-handed circular polarization

Every facet interaction applies a 4×4 Mueller matrix that rotates into the interface's own reference frame, evaluates the parallel and perpendicular Fresnel amplitudes, and — under total internal reflection — applies the phase retardation between them: 

tan(delta / 2) = cos(theta_i) * sqrt(sin^2(theta_i) - (n_t / n_i)^2) / sin^2(theta_i)
delta = delta_p - delta_s

5Uniaxial and biaxial crystal optics

Non-cubic gemstones have an optical indicatrix: the refractive index depends on the propagation direction relative to the crystal's own axes (optics/birefringence/).

Uniaxial (Sapphire, Ruby, Quartz, Zircon, Moissanite, Tourmaline...)

A single optical axis. Incident light splits into an ordinary ray (constant index n_o) and an extraordinary ray whose index depends on the angle θ between the wave normal and the optical axis: 

1 / n_e^2(theta) = cos^2(theta) / n_o^2 + sin^2(theta) / n_e^2

The extraordinary ray's energy also walks off from its wave-normal direction (Poynting vector walk-off), which is what produces visible facet doubling in strongly birefringent stones such as high zircon.

Biaxial (Alexandrite, Topaz, Tanzanite, Peridot, Andalusite...)

Two optical axes and three distinct principal indices n_α ≤ n_β ≤ n_γ. Indicatrix's BiaxialIndicatrix solver evaluates the wave surface for both modes on CPU and, in the current build, in the compute kernel as well — every built-in material, biaxial or uniaxial, is GPU-supported (GemMaterial::gpu_supported is unconditional).

6Directional pleochroic absorption

Pleochroic gemstones show different colour along different crystallographic directions because absorption depends on the electric field's orientation relative to the crystal. Indicatrix evaluates a per-direction absorption tensor via the Beer–Lambert law (optics/raytracer/absorption.rs): 

T(lambda) = exp( - A(lambda, E_hat) * d )

where d is the chord length through the material and E_hat is the ray's electric-field direction.

  • Dichroism (uniaxial materials): distinct ordinary- and extraordinary-ray absorption bands.
  • Trichroism (biaxial materials): three independent absorption spectra along α, β, γ — the mechanism behind Alexandrite's colour change and Tanzanite's trichroism.

7Half-space geometry and concave tools

Instead of tessellating a gemstone into a triangle mesh, Indicatrix represents each facet as an infinite planar half-space with outward unit normal ni and signed distance di (geometry/plane.rs): 

H_i = { x in R^3 | n_i . x + d_i <= 0 }

The gemstone is the intersection of M half-spaces, P = ∩ H_i. Ray intersection is an analytical slab test over all M planes (simd/slab.rs), with no bounding-volume hierarchy and no mesh-junction rounding error. That is the whole stone for a design with flat facets only; concave facets add the tools described next.

Concave facets: tools subtracted from the stone

A concave facet is the surface a shaped tool leaves, so it is not a plane. Indicatrix keeps the planes and subtracts the tools: the stone is P minus the union of the tool volumes Tk (geometry/tool.rs). Each Tk is convex: a ball (a sphere or dimple), a capped cylinder (a cylinder, or a circle as a short one), a cone frustum, or a bicone (a disc, two frusta sharing a rim). A tool can also be swept along or across its axis, which models a tool that reciprocates; the swept volume is what is subtracted. One stone holds at most 128 tool placements (MAX_TOOL_PRIMITIVES).

Because each tool is convex, a ray's interval inside it has a closed form and no iterative solver is needed. For one ray the slab test gives the interval [a, b] inside the planes, and each tool gives its own interval [c, d]. The hit is the first change of "inside the stone" along the ray, past a small distance floor, where inside means inside the planes and outside every tool interval (optics/raytracer/intersect_stone.rs). A tool hit has its own facet id, numbered after the planes, and its normal points into the cavity. With no tools the result is bit for bit the plane-only result, so a design without concave tiers renders as before.

The WGSL kernels know nothing about tools, so a stone with tools is traced on the CPU whatever the compute setting (scene_routes_to_gpu in renderer/gpu_backend/). A remote render carries the tool volumes in the scene (SceneState::tools, wire protocol version 19), so local, remote and combined renders trace the same stone.

8Inclusions and scattering

A stone is not always clear. A material can carry optional silk or haze parameters (a scattering coefficient and a phase asymmetry) that the tracer treats as homogeneous Henyey–Greenstein volumetric scattering (optics/raytracer/scattering/hg.rs). At each bounce the path is either extinguished or scattered inside the stone, decided once for the shared path and reweighted per channel. A scattering point samples the environment directly and combines that with the phase-sampled continuation by a balance-heuristic weight (see the HDR paragraph in the lighting section below), and a frosted facet’s diffuse exit does the same.

9Colour and tone mapping

  • Spectral radiance in each of the 8 channels is integrated against the tabulated CIE 1931 2° colour-matching functions (380–780 nm, 5 nm steps, color/cie1931.rs) to obtain CIE XYZ (optics/raytracer/color.rs).
  • An ACES filmic tone-mapping curve (Narkowicz 2015 fit) is applied to the luminance channel before the vector is scaled back into RGB, avoiding hue shift on saturated dispersion fire (color/space.rs).
  • Out-of-gamut colours are compressed back into the target space; supported output spaces are sRGB, Display P3, and Rec. 2020 (color/gamut.rs, color/space.rs).
  • PNG exports from the desktop studio are 8-bit RGBA; any non-sRGB export (Display P3, Rec. 2020) embeds an ICC profile identifying that colour space, while sRGB exports are written untagged (apps/indicatrix-cut/src/bridge/export_thread/tonemap_png.rs; the ICC profile builder is crates/indicatrix/src/render_setup/icc_profile.rs).

10Environment and lighting models

A ray that misses the stone, or exits it, samples an environment rather than a light list. Thirteen presets (fifteen in builds with the physical-colour feature, which add the two UV lamps) cover the analytic models below, and a real HDR panorama can be loaded in their place (optics/raytracer/environment/):

  • Studio (Daylight, Incandescent, IlluminantA at 2856 K, RingLights, DarkSpotlight) — a charcoal backdrop, one key softbox, one fill, and sixteen ring pinpoints. Its arithmetic is pinned bit for bit by tables in crates/indicatrix/tests/raytracer_tests/golden_regression.rs (which are re-pinned whenever a deliberate physics change moves them).
  • UV lamps (only in builds with the physical-colour feature; UvLamp365, UvLamp395, on the studio model) — the studio rig's geometry lit by a narrow Gaussian line instead of white light: 365 nm and 10 nm wide, or 395 nm and 12 nm wide, with no ambient backdrop and no white balance. A non-fluorescent stone stays nearly dark, and the 395 nm lamp's tail reaches into the violet, so such a stone looks faintly violet under it. Both are traced on the CPU only.
  • Grading tray (IsoHemisphere, formerly “ISO hemisphere”; the preset is labelled D65 hemisphere + head shadow) — the whole upper hemisphere at radiance 1 and nothing below the girdle plane: the reference case, with no directional structure to flatter a cut. It is the scoring light: Optimize and Retarget score every design under it, and it is flat by design, so a tilt video under it shows no sparkle.
  • Light tent, the default preset — dim tent walls, one broad overhead softbox, three black cards on the ring positions away from the key, one small hard spark light, black velvet below the girdle. The black cards are the contrast a jewellery photographer adds so a bright stone reads as a facet pattern instead of a white blur.
  • Light-tent variants (WhiteTray, ShopLights, WindowDaylight) — the same model with other parameters, all lit by a D65 spectrum: a white tray lit from below with bright, perfectly even walls and no cards or spark; a bright diffuse jewellery-shop ceiling with small pinpoint spots; and a dim room lit by one broad window, a single soft key with no cards or spark. The built-in Window daylight view places that key at 30° elevation.
  • Daylight sky (no sun) (DaylightDome) — a clear sky only, brighter at the horizon than at the zenith, with dark ground and no sun disc.
  • Daylight sky and direct sun (DaylightSun) — the same sky plus a real sun, a hard-edged disc 0.27° across that supplies about 82 % of the light at the default key height. It is sampled directly, and a stone flashes with fire whenever a facet catches it.
  • Contrast view (Aset) — an ASET-style diagnostic view that colours the sky by elevation — green from 0° to 45°, red from 45° to 75°, blue from 75° to 90° overhead, and black below the horizon — with no white balance, for seeing where in the stone the light returns from. Its figures are scored as the Grading tray.

The lit models also darken every exit direction that falls inside the observer's own head-shadow cone — the term that gives a face-up stone its dark table reflections; the readouts follow the same head-shadow cone and count every lit direction under a lit model, whereas under a Studio rig brilliance is measured against that rig's own lamps. The Studio rig ignores the observer. The size of the head shadow is the Head shadow setting, 0–30° (default 16°, about a head at arm’s length; 0 switches it off). It changes the brilliance, windowing and extinction figures as well as the picture, so a wider shadow lowers face-up brilliance; the Studio rigs, the UV lamps, the contrast view and an HDR map ignore it. Radiances are chosen so that at exposure 1 the ambient terms land near middle grey after the ACES curve and only a direct reflection of a light source clips to white.

In place of an analytic preset, a real HDR panorama can be loaded instead (EnvironmentSource::HdrMap, renderer/env_map.rs): a 2D piecewise-constant inverse-CDF sampler draws directions proportional to the map's own solid-angle-corrected luminance, so samples land where the panorama is actually bright rather than being spent on a typical capture's dark majority. The solid-angle Jacobian is written symmetrically around both poles (sin(min(v, 1−v) · π)) so texels near the north and south pole get equally well-conditioned densities. The same distribution backs a genuine next-event-estimation path on both CPU and the WGSL compute kernel: volumetric scattering inside the stone and a frosted facet's diffusely-sampled exit each sample the environment directly and combine it with the phase/BSDF-sampled continuation via a Veach-style balance-heuristic MIS weight, rather than waiting for a bounce to randomly find a bright pixel.

The main light is set by two angles. Light azimuth runs 0–360° (default 48°) and light elevation 10–90° (default 72°). They move the light tent’s softbox (its black cards turn with it), the sun of the direct-sun preset, the glow of the sky-only preset, the window of Window daylight and the Studio key softbox. The Grading tray does not depend on them, since the whole hemisphere is lit evenly, and an HDR map ignores them. Studio exposure scales the light of every built-in preset from 0.4× to 2.5× (default 1×) and is likewise not applied to an HDR map.

A view stores a lighting preset together with the light direction, the exposure and the camera distance, and for some also the camera pose. Seven ship with the app: Studio Softbox, Daylight Bright, Dramatic Spotlight, Light Tent, Grading tray (the camera looks almost straight down, as a grader would), Daylight Sun and Window daylight (the window at 30° elevation). Your own views are saved beside them, and each can be marked for the export dialog so that one export renders the stone under several lightings.

Separately from the environment, a backdrop card can be placed behind the stone: BACKDROP_GREY (0.23, tone-mapping to roughly sRGB 160) or BACKDROP_WHITE (8.0, a light box). Only the primary camera ray sees it — the stone's own optics never do — so light leaking through a window or a poorly-angled pavilion stays as dark as the real ground behind it instead of picking up a glow the card would otherwise cast. The six-material comparison in the overview’s renders section sits on the grey card.

11GPU equivalence harness

The optional GPU backend (crates/indicatrix/src/renderer/gpu) runs a hand-maintained WGSL port of the transport physics, not a compiled translation: the shader units under crates/indicatrix/src/renderer/shaders are concatenated by build.rs and dispatched as a compute kernel. It is checked against the CPU reference by a tiered harness rather than a single pass/fail comparison:

TierScopeStandard
Tier 1 RNG seed hashing, integer state, sub-pixel jitter indices (renderer/gpu/rng_check.rs) Bit-exact
Tier 2 Individual ported functions — Sellmeier evaluation, Fresnel/TIR, Stokes-Mueller rotation (renderer/gpu/transport_check/), and ray-plane polyhedron intersection (renderer/gpu/polyhedron_check.rs); renderer/gpu/ulp.rs holds the shared ULP-distance helper both use Per-function ULP budget (case-bank match for the intersection test, whose result is a discrete hit record rather than a smooth scalar)
Tier 3 Full rendered images, CPU vs. GPU, for a representative subset of the built-in materials, about 9 of the 32 (Spinel, Zircon, Tourmaline, Quartz, Rutile, Synthetic Moissanite, Alexandrite, Topaz, Tanzanite; renderer/gpu/estimator_check/image_comparison.rs) Statistical (Welford mean/variance) equivalence
Furnace anchor A stone in a uniform, wavelength-independent radiance environment — a "white furnace" test (renderer/gpu/furnace_check.rs) Per-tuple ULP budget against the known analytical result

Every WGSL shader unit is also parsed with naga in renderer/gpu/shader_validation_tests.rs, independently of the numerical comparisons above. A cross-backend merge test renders one sample range on the CPU and the next on the GPU, merges them the production way and checks the result statistically against an independent CPU reference (renderer/gpu/merge_tests.rs; every backend drops a non-finite sample but still counts it, under one shared rule). The example harness can be run locally against your own GPU adapter: 

cargo run --profile probe -p indicatrix --features gpu --example gpu_equivalence_harness
